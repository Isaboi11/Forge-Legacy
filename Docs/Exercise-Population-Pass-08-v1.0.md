## Section 1 — Leg Swing

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Leg Swing |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | STRETCH_DYNAMIC |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HIP_FLEXORS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS |

**ABOUT**
> The Leg Swing is a dynamic mobility exercise performed by swinging one leg forward and back, or side to side, while balancing on the other leg. It moves the hip through a wide range of motion using momentum. It is performed continuously rather than held statically.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Leg Swing builds hip mobility and warms up the hip joint through dynamic, momentum-driven movement. It is well suited as a pre-training warm-up before running, squatting, or lower-body work. It also helps athletes assess their available hip range before loading it. It requires no equipment and can be performed almost anywhere.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand balanced on one leg, holding onto something stable if needed.
2. Swing your other leg forward and back in a controlled arc.
3. Gradually increase the range as your hip warms up.
4. Keep your torso upright throughout the swings.
5. Repeat for the set, then switch legs.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your standing leg stable throughout the swings
> — Let the range grow gradually rather than forcing it early
> — Keep your torso upright rather than leaning back
> — Keep the swing controlled rather than letting momentum take over

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The torso leans back to help the leg swing higher
> — The standing leg wobbles instead of staying stable
> — The range is forced beyond what feels controlled

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Walking Knee Hug, Hip Circles

**ABOUT**
> Arm Circles is a dynamic mobility exercise performed by rotating both arms in large circles at the shoulder. It moves the shoulder joint through a wide range of motion. It is performed continuously rather than held statically.

> Arm Circles builds shoulder mobility and warms up the shoulder joint before upper-body or overhead training. It is well suited as a quick pre-training warm-up requiring no equipment. It also helps athletes assess shoulder range before loading it with pressing or pulling work. It is accessible to nearly any training level.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart and arms extended out to the sides.
2. Rotate both arms in small circles.
3. Gradually increase the size of the circles.

> — Keep your shoulders relaxed rather than shrugged up
> — Move at a steady, controlled pace

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The shoulders shrug up toward the ears
> — The circles become uneven in size between arms
> — The movement speeds up into an uncontrolled flail

## Section 3 — Walking Knee Hug

| Field | Value |
|---|---|

| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | STRETCH_DYNAMIC |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HIP_FLEXORS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | GLUTES |

**ABOUT**
> The Walking Knee Hug is a dynamic mobility exercise performed by stepping forward, pulling one knee toward the chest, then stepping into the next rep. It moves the hip through flexion while walking forward. It combines a stretch with locomotion.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Walking Knee Hug builds hip and hamstring mobility while warming up the body through forward movement. It is well suited as a pre-training warm-up before running or lower-body training. It also reinforces balance and coordination while moving, unlike a stretch held in place. It requires no equipment beyond open floor space.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand tall and step forward with one leg.
2. Lift your opposite knee up and pull it toward your chest with both hands.
3. Briefly balance on your standing leg while holding the knee in.
4. Release your knee and step forward into the next rep.
5. Continue alternating legs as you move forward.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Stand tall on your standing leg rather than hunching forward
> — Pull your knee in firmly but without straining
> — Keep a steady walking pace between reps
> — Keep your core braced to stay balanced

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The torso hunches forward to help pull the knee in
> — The standing leg wobbles instead of staying stable
> — The pace rushes instead of staying controlled

**Progressions:** World's Greatest Stretch
**Regressions:** Leg Swing
**Alternatives:** Inchworm

| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, CORE |

**ABOUT**
> The Inchworm is a dynamic mobility exercise performed by bending forward to place the hands on the floor, walking them out to a plank, then walking the feet back in. It moves the hamstrings, hips, and shoulders through a full-body flow. It combines a stretch with light core and shoulder activation.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Inchworm builds hamstring and hip mobility along with light shoulder and core activation in a single flowing movement. It is well suited as a complete warm-up before full-body or lower-body training. It also reinforces the plank position used in many other exercises. It requires no equipment beyond open floor space.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet hip-width apart.
2. Bend forward and place your hands on the floor.
3. Walk your hands forward until your body is in a plank position.
4. Hold the plank briefly, keeping your core braced.
5. Walk your feet forward to meet your hands.
6. Stand back up to the starting position.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your legs as straight as comfortable during the hand walk-out
> — Keep your core braced during the plank position
> — Move at a controlled pace rather than rushing the walk-out
> — Bend your knees slightly if your hamstrings are very tight

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips sag during the plank portion
> — The lower back rounds aggressively during the forward bend
> — The steps become rushed instead of controlled

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.

**ABOUT**
> The Dynamic Lunge with Reach is a dynamic mobility exercise performed by stepping into a lunge and reaching the arms overhead or to the side. It moves the hips, shoulders, and spine through a combined flow. It is performed continuously while moving forward.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Dynamic Lunge with Reach builds hip and upper-body mobility together in a single flowing movement. It is well suited as a warm-up before full-body or lower-body training. It also reinforces the coordination needed to move smoothly between a lower-body position and an upper-body reach. It requires no equipment beyond open floor space.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand tall and step forward into a lunge.

> — Reach fully overhead rather than a partial reach
> — Keep your torso upright as you reach
> — Move at a controlled pace between steps

> — The front knee drifts forward past the toes
> — The reach is rushed instead of held briefly at the bottom
> — The torso leans forward instead of staying upright

## Section 11 — Shoulder CARs

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Shoulder CARs |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | JOINT_MOBILITY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | SHOULDERS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | None |

**ABOUT**
> Shoulder CARs is a joint mobility exercise performed by moving one arm through the largest possible circle at the shoulder while keeping the rest of the body still. It trains active control through the shoulder's full range of motion. CARs stands for controlled articular rotations.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> Shoulder CARs builds active control through the full range of the shoulder joint, rather than just passive flexibility. It is well suited as a warm-up before overhead or pressing-heavy training. It also helps identify and gradually improve any restricted area within the shoulder's range. It requires no equipment and can be performed almost anywhere.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand tall with one arm extended out to the side.
2. Brace your core and keep your torso still.
3. Rotate your arm through the largest controlled circle possible.
4. Move slowly enough to control every part of the circle.
5. Continue for several rotations, then switch arms.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Move as slowly as needed to stay in full control
> — Keep your torso still throughout the rotation
> — Make the circle as large as you can control, not as large as possible
> — Pause briefly if you find a restricted point in the range

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The torso twists or leans to help complete the circle
> — The movement speeds up, losing control through part of the range
> — The circle shrinks to avoid a restricted area instead of working through it

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.

## Section 13 — Deep Squat Hold

| Field | Value |

| Movement Pattern | JOINT_MOBILITY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HIP_FLEXORS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | QUADS, CALVES |

**ABOUT**
> The Deep Squat Hold is a joint mobility exercise performed by sinking into the bottom of a squat and holding the position. It opens the hips, ankles, and lower back through a sustained deep-flexion position. It is held rather than moved through repetitions.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Deep Squat Hold builds hip, ankle, and lower-back mobility in the deepest position the body can reach in a squat pattern. It is well suited for athletes who have built a base of hip and ankle mobility and want to deepen it further. It also helps assess how much squat depth is currently available before loading it. It is one of the more demanding joint-mobility positions in the catalog.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet slightly wider than shoulder width.
2. Lower into the bottom of a squat, hips below your knees.
3. Rest your elbows against the insides of your knees.
4. Press your knees outward gently with your elbows.
5. Keep your heels flat on the floor.
6. Hold the position while breathing steadily.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your heels flat on the floor throughout the hold
> — Press your knees out gently rather than forcing them
> — Keep your chest as tall as your mobility allows
> — Breathe steadily rather than holding your breath

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The heels lift off the floor to reach the position
> — The lower back rounds aggressively at the bottom
> — The position is forced rather than eased into

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** 90/90 Hip Switch
**Alternatives:** Pigeon Pose

| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | BACK |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | SHOULDERS |

**ABOUT**
> The Scapular Push-Up is a joint mobility exercise performed in a plank position, moving only the shoulder blades together and apart without bending the elbows. It trains control of the shoulder blades against the rib cage. It is the most basic shoulder-blade mobility drill in the catalog.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Scapular Push-Up builds control of the shoulder blades, a foundational skill for healthy pressing and pulling mechanics. It is well suited as a warm-up before upper-body training or as a regression for athletes still learning shoulder-blade awareness. It also reinforces the same scapular control used in push-ups and overhead pressing. It requires no equipment beyond a flat surface.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Start in a plank position on your hands or forearms.
2. Keep your arms straight and elbows locked if on your hands.
3. Let your shoulder blades sink together, lowering your chest slightly.
4. Push your shoulder blades apart, rounding your upper back.
5. Continue moving between the two positions without bending your elbows.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your elbows locked throughout if performing this on your hands
> — Move only your shoulder blades, not your hips or spine
> — Move slowly and with control between each position
> — Keep your core braced throughout the movement

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The elbows bend to assist the movement
> — The hips sag or pike instead of staying in a straight line
> — The movement becomes too small to feel the shoulder blades working

**Progressions:** Thoracic Rotation
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.

**ABOUT**
> The Light Treadmill Walk is a joint mobility exercise performed by walking at an easy pace to gently move the major joints of the body. It raises body temperature and circulation without significant exertion. It is used as a general warm-up rather than a targeted stretch.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Light Treadmill Walk builds general blood flow and joint readiness before more targeted mobility or strength work begins. It is well suited as the first few minutes of any warm-up sequence, particularly before a heavier training session. It also offers a low-impact option for easing into movement after time away from training. It requires only a treadmill or open walking space.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Set the treadmill to an easy, comfortable walking pace.
2. Stand tall with a relaxed posture.
3. Swing your arms naturally as you walk.
4. Continue at an easy pace for several minutes.
5. Gradually increase your pace slightly if preparing for more intense training.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep the pace easy rather than challenging
> — Let your arms swing naturally with your stride
