## Section 1 — Leg Extension

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Leg Extension |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | KNEE_ISOLATION |
| Equipment Tag(s) | MACHINE |
| Primary Muscle(s) | QUADS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | None |

**ABOUT**
> The Leg Extension is a machine-based knee-isolation exercise that trains the quads through a seated leg-straightening motion. It uses a resistance pad positioned across the shins. It isolates knee extension without involving the hips.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Leg Extension builds quad strength and size in a focused, single-joint movement. It is well suited for adding targeted quad volume after compound squat and lunge work. It also allows precise load control, which is useful for rehabilitation or working around a hip limitation. It is a staple accessory exercise in hypertrophy and lower-body programs.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Sit on the machine with your back against the pad and shins behind the resistance pad.
2. Adjust the pad so it rests just above your ankles.
3. Grip the side handles and brace your core.
4. Extend your knees to straighten your legs against the resistance.
5. Pause briefly at full extension.
6. Lower the weight back down under control.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your back flat against the pad throughout
> — Extend until your legs are straight without locking your knees aggressively
> — Lower the weight slowly rather than letting it drop
> — Keep your hips still as your knees extend

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips lift off the seat to help move the weight
> — The weight drops quickly on the way down instead of lowering under control
> — The range of motion shortens, stopping short of full extension

**Progressions:** Sissy Squat
**Regressions:** Banded Leg Extension
**Alternatives:** Leg Press, Wall Sit

## Section 2 — Seated Leg Curl

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Seated Leg Curl |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | KNEE_ISOLATION |
| Equipment Tag(s) | MACHINE |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | None |

**ABOUT**
> The Seated Leg Curl is a machine-based knee-isolation exercise that trains the hamstrings through a seated leg-curling motion. It uses a resistance pad positioned across the lower legs. It isolates knee flexion without involving the hips.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Seated Leg Curl builds hamstring strength and size in a focused, single-joint movement. It is well suited for balancing quad-dominant training with direct hamstring work. It also allows precise load control for athletes managing a hamstring injury history. It complements deadlift and hip-hinge work as part of a complete lower-body program.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Sit on the machine with your back against the pad and legs extended.
2. Position the resistance pad against the back of your lower legs.
3. Grip the side handles and brace your core.
4. Curl your legs down and under the seat against the resistance.
5. Pause briefly at the bottom of the curl.
6. Return your legs back to the starting position under control.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your back flat against the pad throughout
> — Curl through a full range of motion rather than a short partial range
> — Lower back to the start slowly rather than letting it snap back
> — Keep your hips still as your legs curl

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips lift off the seat to help move the weight
> — The weight snaps back quickly instead of returning under control
> — The range of motion shortens, stopping well short of full flexion

**Progressions:** Nordic Hamstring Curl
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Lying Leg Curl, Barbell Romanian Deadlift

## Section 3 — Box Jump

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Box Jump |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | PLYOMETRIC_LOWER |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT, BENCH |
| Primary Muscle(s) | QUADS, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, CALVES |

**ABOUT**
> The Box Jump is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by jumping explosively from the floor onto an elevated box. It trains the glutes, quads, and hamstrings for explosive lower-body power. It requires a stable box or platform.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Box Jump builds explosive lower-body power that carries over to sprinting, jumping, and change-of-direction sports. It teaches the body to produce force quickly rather than slowly under a heavy load. It is well suited for athletes and lifters looking to add a power component to their training. It also reinforces safe landing mechanics under control.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand facing the box with feet shoulder-width apart.
2. Bend your knees and swing your arms back to load the jump.
3. Swing your arms forward and jump explosively onto the box.
4. Land softly on the box with bent knees.
5. Stand fully upright on top of the box.
6. Step back down to the floor under control.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Land softly with bent knees to absorb the impact
> — Choose a box height you can land on with control
> — Reset your stance fully between reps
> — Step down rather than jumping back down to the floor

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The knees cave inward on landing
> — The landing is stiff and loud instead of soft and controlled
> — The box height is too aggressive for a controlled landing

**Progressions:** Single-Leg Box Jump
**Regressions:** Pogo Hop
**Alternatives:** Broad Jump, Tuck Jump

## Section 4 — Jump Squat

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Jump Squat |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | PLYOMETRIC_LOWER |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | QUADS, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | CALVES |

**ABOUT**
> The Jump Squat is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by squatting down and exploding upward into a jump. It trains the glutes, quads, and hamstrings for explosive lower-body power. It is performed in place without an external box or platform.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Jump Squat builds explosive lower-body power using only body weight. It develops the rate of force production that carries over to sprinting and jumping sports. It is well suited for athletes adding a power component to their training without needing equipment. It also reinforces safe landing mechanics on every rep.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart.
2. Bend your knees and hips to lower into a squat.
3. Swing your arms back as you lower.
4. Drive explosively upward into a jump.
5. Land softly with bent knees back in the squat position.
6. Reset your stance before the next rep.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Land softly with bent knees on every rep
> — Drive through your whole foot as you jump
> — Reset your stance fully between reps
> — Keep your chest tall throughout the movement

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The knees cave inward on the landing
> — The landing is stiff instead of absorbing the impact through the knees and hips
> — The depth of the squat shortens as fatigue builds

**Progressions:** Depth Jump
**Regressions:** Pogo Hop
**Alternatives:** Box Jump, Lateral Bound

## Section 5 — Farmer Carry

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Farmer Carry |
| Category | FULL_BODY |
| Movement Pattern | CARRY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | DUMBBELL, KETTLEBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | FOREARMS, CORE |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | SHOULDERS, GLUTES |

**ABOUT**
> The Farmer Carry is a loaded carry exercise performed by holding a heavy weight in each hand and walking a fixed distance. It trains grip, core, and total-body stability under load. It uses a dumbbell or kettlebell in each hand.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Farmer Carry builds grip strength and total-body stability that carries over to everyday lifting and carrying tasks. It develops core bracing under load without requiring a specific lift pattern. It is well suited for athletes and lifters looking to build functional, real-world strength. It also serves as an efficient conditioning tool since it challenges the whole body at once.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand between two weights, one on each side.
2. Bend at your hips and knees to pick up both weights.
3. Stand tall with a weight in each hand at your sides.
4. Brace your core and pull your shoulders back.
5. Walk forward in a straight line with controlled steps.
6. Set both weights down with control at the end of the distance.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your shoulders pulled back and chest tall throughout
> — Brace your core with every step
> — Take controlled, even steps rather than rushing
> — Keep the weights at your sides rather than letting them swing forward

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The shoulders round forward as the walk continues
> — The pace speeds up and steps become uneven near the end
> — The core relaxes, letting the torso lean from side to side

**Progressions:** Yoke Carry
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Suitcase Carry, Trap Bar Carry

## Section 6 — Suitcase Carry

| Movement Pattern | CARRY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | DUMBBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | CORE, FOREARMS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | SHOULDERS |

**ABOUT**
> The Suitcase Carry is a loaded carry exercise performed by holding a single weight at one side and walking a fixed distance. It trains the core's ability to resist side-bending under an uneven load. It uses one dumbbell held like a suitcase.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Suitcase Carry builds core strength specifically for resisting lateral bending under load. It carries over to real-world tasks where weight is carried unevenly, such as carrying a bag on one side. It is well suited for athletes and lifters looking to train core stability in a single-sided, practical way. It also exposes and helps correct side-to-side core strength imbalances.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand next to a weight placed at your side.
2. Bend at your hips and knees to pick up the weight with one hand.
3. Stand tall holding the weight at your side.
4. Brace your core to keep your torso upright.
5. Walk forward in a straight line with controlled steps.
6. Set the weight down with control, then repeat on the other side.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your torso upright rather than leaning toward the weight
> — Brace your core with every step
> — Keep your shoulders level throughout the walk
> — Take controlled, even steps rather than rushing

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The torso leans toward the loaded side to compensate
> — The shoulders drop unevenly during the walk
> — The pace speeds up and steps become uneven near the end

**Progressions:** Overhead Carry
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Farmer Carry, Front-Loaded Carry

## Section 7 — Turkish Get-Up

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Turkish Get-Up |
| Category | FULL_BODY |
| Movement Pattern | EXPLOSIVE_FULLBODY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | KETTLEBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | FULL_BODY |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | CORE, SHOULDERS |

**ABOUT**
> The Turkish Get-Up is a full-body exercise performed by moving from lying on the floor to a standing position while holding a kettlebell overhead. It trains total-body strength, stability, and coordination through a slow, controlled sequence. It is performed deliberately rather than explosively.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Turkish Get-Up builds shoulder stability, core control, and total-body coordination in a single, demanding sequence. It teaches the body to maintain stability through a wide range of changing positions. It is well suited for lifters and athletes who want a comprehensive movement-control challenge. It also reinforces the overhead stability used in pressing and carrying exercises.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Lie on your back holding a kettlebell overhead with one arm, arm fully extended.
2. Bend the same-side knee and plant that foot on the floor.
3. Roll onto your opposite forearm, then your hand, keeping the kettlebell overhead.
4. Lift your hips and sweep your back leg under your body into a kneeling position.
5. Drive up from the kneeling position to a standing position, keeping the kettlebell overhead throughout.
6. Reverse the sequence step by step to return to the floor.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your eyes on the kettlebell throughout the entire sequence
> — Move slowly and deliberately through each position
> — Keep the working arm fully locked out overhead at all times
> — Brace your core before transitioning between positions

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The eyes drift away from the kettlebell during a transition
> — The overhead arm bends or loses lockout during a position change
> — The sequence rushes through transitions instead of pausing to find stability

**Progressions:** Clean and Press
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** None — no genuine same-intent, same-difficulty substitute exists for this exercise's deliberate, multi-position control demand within the launch catalog.

## Section 8 — Burpee

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Burpee |
| Category | FULL_BODY |
| Movement Pattern | EXPLOSIVE_FULLBODY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | FULL_BODY |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | CHEST, QUADS |

**ABOUT**
> The Burpee is a full-body bodyweight exercise that combines a squat, a push-up, and a jump into one continuous sequence. It trains the chest, shoulders, core, and legs while elevating the heart rate. It requires no equipment.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Burpee builds full-body conditioning and explosive power in a single bodyweight movement. It is one of the most efficient exercises for combining strength and cardiovascular demand. It is well suited for circuit training, conditioning programs, and any session needing a high-intensity bodyweight option. It also requires no equipment, making it accessible anywhere.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart.
2. Bend down and place your hands on the floor in front of you.
3. Jump your feet back into a plank position.
4. Lower your chest to the floor in a push-up.
5. Press back up and jump your feet forward to your hands.
6. Stand up and jump explosively into the air.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your core braced through the plank and push-up phases
> — Land softly with bent knees after the jump
> — Move at a steady, sustainable pace across reps
> — Keep your body in a straight line during the plank phase

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips sag during the plank or push-up phase
> — The landing after the jump is stiff instead of absorbed through the knees
> — The push-up phase is skipped or rushed without full range of motion

**Progressions:** Wall Ball
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Medicine Ball Slam, Battle Ropes

## Section 9 — Pigeon Pose

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Pigeon Pose |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | STRETCH_STATIC |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HIP_FLEXORS |

**ABOUT**
> Pigeon Pose is a static stretch performed by bending the front leg in front of the body and extending the back leg behind, opening the hip of the front leg. It stretches the hip external rotators and glutes. It is held for a sustained period rather than moved through repetitions.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> Pigeon Pose builds hip mobility by lengthening the muscles that rotate the hip outward. It is well suited for athletes and lifters who sit for long periods or whose training involves heavy squatting and hinging. It also supports recovery between training sessions by easing tightness built up in the hips. It pairs well with lower-body strength work in a complete program.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Start on your hands and knees.
2. Bring one knee forward and place it behind your wrist, with your shin angled across your body.
3. Extend your back leg straight behind you.
4. Square your hips as much as comfortable and lower your torso forward.
5. Hold the position while breathing steadily.
6. Switch sides and repeat.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Square your hips toward the front rather than letting them rotate
> — Breathe steadily rather than holding your breath
> — Ease into the stretch gradually rather than forcing the position
> — Support your weight on your hands if the stretch feels too intense

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips rotate to one side instead of staying square
> — The breath is held instead of staying steady through the hold
> — The position is forced deeper than is comfortable

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Figure-Four Stretch
**Alternatives:** Child's Pose

## Section 10 — Standing Hamstring Stretch

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Standing Hamstring Stretch |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | STRETCH_STATIC |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | None |

**ABOUT**
> The Standing Hamstring Stretch is a static stretch performed standing, hinging forward at the hips to lengthen the hamstrings. It stretches the back of the legs while the athlete remains upright. It can be performed with a straight or slightly bent front leg.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Standing Hamstring Stretch builds hamstring flexibility that supports hip-hinge movements like the Deadlift and Barbell Romanian Deadlift. It is well suited as part of a warm-up before lower-body training or a cool-down afterward. It also helps offset the tightness that builds from long periods of sitting. It requires no equipment and can be done almost anywhere.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet hip-width apart.
2. Extend one leg slightly forward with a small bend in the knee.
3. Hinge forward at your hips, keeping your back flat.
4. Reach toward your extended foot until you feel a stretch in your hamstring.
5. Hold the position while breathing steadily.
6. Return to standing and switch legs.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Hinge from your hips rather than rounding your lower back
> — Breathe steadily throughout the hold
> — Ease into the stretch gradually rather than bouncing
> — Keep a slight bend in your front knee rather than locking it

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The lower back rounds instead of staying flat during the hinge
> — The stretch is bounced into instead of held steadily
> — The knee locks out fully, adding unnecessary strain

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Seated Forward Fold
**Alternatives:** None — no genuine same-intent, different-execution substitute targeting this specific stretch exists within the launch catalog.

## Section 11 — Cat-Cow

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Cat-Cow |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | STRETCH_DYNAMIC |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | LOWER_BACK |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | CORE |

**ABOUT**
> Cat-Cow is a dynamic mobility exercise performed on the hands and knees, alternating between arching and rounding the spine. It moves the entire spine through flexion and extension. It is performed slowly and continuously rather than held statically.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> Cat-Cow builds spinal mobility by moving the back through its full range of motion in both directions. It is well suited as a warm-up before training or a gentle mobility drill on a rest day. It also helps relieve general back stiffness from long periods of sitting or standing. It is accessible to nearly any training level.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Start on your hands and knees with your back in a neutral position.
2. Drop your belly and lift your chest and tailbone, arching your spine.
3. Hold the arched position briefly.
4. Round your spine, tucking your chin and tailbone under.
5. Hold the rounded position briefly.
6. Repeat the sequence, moving smoothly between the two positions.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Move slowly between each position rather than rushing
> — Let the movement flow from your spine rather than just your shoulders
> — Breathe in as you arch and out as you round
> — Keep your hands and knees still as your spine moves

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The movement happens only at the neck instead of through the whole spine
> — The transitions are rushed instead of moving smoothly
> — The hands or knees shift position during the movement

**Progressions:** World's Greatest Stretch
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Hip Circles

## Section 12 — World's Greatest Stretch

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | World's Greatest Stretch |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | STRETCH_DYNAMIC |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | FULL_BODY |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HIP_FLEXORS, HAMSTRINGS |

**ABOUT**
> The World's Greatest Stretch is a dynamic mobility exercise that combines a lunge, a rotation, and a hamstring stretch into one flowing sequence. It moves the hips, spine, and hamstrings through a full range of motion. It is performed standing, stepping into a deep lunge.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The World's Greatest Stretch builds mobility across multiple joints in a single efficient sequence. It is well suited as a complete warm-up before lower-body or full-body training. It also reinforces the coordination needed to move smoothly between positions under control. It is commonly used to prepare the body for squat- and hinge-pattern training.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Step forward into a deep lunge with your back leg extended behind you.
2. Place both hands on the floor inside your front foot.
3. Rotate your torso and reach one arm toward the ceiling.
4. Return your hand to the floor and straighten your front leg to stretch your hamstring.
5. Bend your front knee again and return to the lunge position.
6. Step back to standing and repeat on the other side.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Move through each position slowly and with control
> — Keep your front knee tracking over your front foot during the lunge
> — Rotate from your torso rather than just twisting your arm
> — Breathe steadily throughout the sequence

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The front knee drifts inward during the lunge position
> — The rotation comes from the arm alone instead of the torso
> — The sequence is rushed, skipping the controlled transitions between positions

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Cat-Cow
**Alternatives:** Dynamic Lunge with Reach, Walking Knee Hug

## Section 13 — 90/90 Hip Switch

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | 90/90 Hip Switch |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | JOINT_MOBILITY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HIP_FLEXORS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | GLUTES |

**ABOUT**
> The 90/90 Hip Switch is a joint mobility exercise performed seated, rotating both hips from one side to the other while keeping both knees bent at 90 degrees. It moves the hips through internal and external rotation. It is performed slowly and with control.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The 90/90 Hip Switch builds hip rotation mobility that supports squatting, lunging, and rotational sports movements. It is well suited as a mobility drill before lower-body training or as a standalone hip-health practice. It also helps identify which side has more restricted hip rotation. It requires no equipment and can be done on the floor.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Sit on the floor with both knees bent at 90 degrees, one in front and one to the side.
2. Keep your chest tall and hands on the floor for support.
3. Rotate both hips to switch the front and back leg to the opposite side.
4. Lower both legs to the floor on the new side.
5. Pause briefly in the new position.
6. Repeat the switch back to the other side.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Move slowly through the rotation rather than swinging the legs
> — Keep your chest tall throughout the movement
> — Use your hands on the floor for support as needed
> — Pause briefly on each side to settle into the position

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The legs swing quickly instead of rotating under control
> — The chest collapses forward during the switch
> — Only one hip does the work while the other stays passive

**Progressions:** Deep Squat Hold
**Regressions:** Hip CARs
**Alternatives:** Hip Circles

## Section 14 — Thoracic Rotation

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Thoracic Rotation |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | JOINT_MOBILITY |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | BACK |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | CORE |

**ABOUT**
> Thoracic Rotation is a joint mobility exercise performed on the hands and knees, rotating the upper spine while one hand reaches toward the ceiling. It moves the thoracic spine through rotation. It is performed slowly on each side.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> Thoracic Rotation builds upper-back mobility that supports rotational sports movements and overhead pressing. It is well suited as a mobility drill before upper-body training or as a standalone practice for desk-bound stiffness. It also helps offset the limited thoracic rotation that comes from long periods of sitting. It requires no equipment.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Start on your hands and knees with your back flat.
2. Place one hand behind your head.
3. Rotate your torso, bringing that elbow down toward your opposite arm.
4. Reverse the rotation, opening your chest and reaching your elbow toward the ceiling.
5. Follow the movement with your eyes as your elbow rotates up.
6. Repeat the rotation, then switch sides.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Rotate from your upper back rather than your lower back
> — Keep your hips still throughout the rotation
> — Move slowly rather than rushing the rotation
> — Follow your elbow with your eyes to encourage a fuller rotation

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips rotate along with the upper back instead of staying still
> — The movement is rushed instead of controlled
> — The rotation stays shallow instead of reaching the available range

**Progressions:** None — no genuinely harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Scapular Push-Up
**Alternatives:** Standing Spinal Twist

## Section 15 — Foam Rolling Quads

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Foam Rolling Quads |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | FOAM_ROLL |
| Equipment Tag(s) | FOAM_ROLLER |
| Primary Muscle(s) | QUADS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | None |

**ABOUT**
> Foam Rolling Quads is a self-myofascial release exercise performed by rolling the front of the thigh over a foam roller. It applies sustained pressure to the quad muscles. It is performed slowly, pausing on tender areas.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> Foam Rolling Quads helps reduce muscle tightness and tension in the front of the thigh after training. It is well suited as part of a cool-down after lower-body training or as a standalone recovery practice. It also supports general lower-body recovery between sessions. It requires only a foam roller.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Lie face down with the foam roller positioned under your thighs.
2. Support your upper body on your forearms.
3. Brace your core and lift your hips slightly off the floor.
4. Roll slowly from just above your knees up toward your hips.
5. Pause on any tender areas for a few breaths.
6. Continue rolling for the desired duration.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Roll slowly rather than quickly back and forth
> — Pause on tender spots rather than rolling straight through them
> — Breathe steadily throughout the rolling
> — Keep your core braced to control your body weight on the roller

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The rolling speeds up instead of staying slow and controlled
> — The lower back sags instead of staying supported by the core
> — The pressure is rushed past tender areas instead of pausing there

**Progressions:** None — self-myofascial release has no genuine difficulty ladder within the launch catalog.
**Regressions:** None — self-myofascial release has no genuine difficulty ladder within the launch catalog.

## Section 16 — Foam Rolling Upper Back

**ABOUT**
> Foam Rolling Upper Back is a self-myofascial release exercise performed by rolling the upper back over a foam roller while lying on the floor. It applies sustained pressure to the muscles along the upper spine. It is performed slowly with the knees bent and feet planted.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> Foam Rolling Upper Back helps reduce tightness in the upper back and improves comfort during overhead and pressing movements. It is well suited as part of a warm-up before upper-body training or a cool-down afterward. It also supports recovery from long periods of sitting at a desk. It requires only a foam roller.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Lie on your back with the foam roller positioned under your upper back.
2. Bend your knees and plant your feet on the floor.
3. Cross your arms over your chest or support your head with your hands.
4. Lift your hips slightly and roll slowly from your shoulder blades down toward your mid-back.
5. Pause on any tender areas for a few breaths.
6. Continue rolling for the desired duration.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Roll slowly rather than quickly back and forth
> — Pause on tender spots rather than rolling straight through them
> — Keep your core braced to control your body weight on the roller
> — Avoid rolling directly over your lower back

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The rolling extends down into the lower back
> — The neck strains forward to support the head instead of using the hands
> — The rolling speeds up instead of staying slow and controlled

## Section 17 — Box Breathing

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Box Breathing |
| Category | MOBILITY |
| Movement Pattern | BREATHWORK |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | CORE |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | None |

**ABOUT**
> Box Breathing is a breathwork exercise performed by inhaling, holding, exhaling, and holding again for equal counts. It trains controlled, even breathing. It is performed seated or lying down in a quiet position.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> Box Breathing builds the ability to calm the nervous system through controlled breath patterns. It is well suited for use before a stressful event, after an intense training session, or as a standalone relaxation practice. It also supports recovery by shifting the body out of a high-stress state. It requires no equipment and can be practiced anywhere.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Sit or lie in a comfortable, quiet position.
2. Exhale fully to begin with empty lungs.
3. Inhale slowly through your nose for a count of four.
4. Hold your breath for a count of four.
5. Exhale slowly through your mouth for a count of four.
6. Hold the empty position for a count of four before repeating.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep each count as close to equal length as possible
> — Breathe through your nose on the inhale for more control
> — Relax your shoulders throughout the practice
> — Start with a shorter count if four feels difficult to sustain

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The breath becomes shallow instead of staying slow and full
> — The counts become uneven between the four phases
> — The shoulders and chest tense up rather than staying relaxed

**Progressions:** None — breathwork has no genuine difficulty ladder within the launch catalog.
**Regressions:** None — breathwork has no genuine difficulty ladder within the launch catalog.
**Alternatives:** Diaphragmatic Breathing, 4-7-8 Breathing
