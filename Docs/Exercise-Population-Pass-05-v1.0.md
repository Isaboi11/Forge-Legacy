## Section 1 — Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | DUMBBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | LOWER_BACK |

**ABOUT**
> The Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift is a hip-hinge exercise performed holding a dumbbell in each hand, lowering and raising the weight along the legs. It trains the hamstrings and glutes through a controlled stretch under load. It is a lighter-equipment alternative to the barbell version.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift builds hamstring and glute strength while allowing each side to work independently. It is well suited for lifters training at home or anyone without barbell access. It also serves as an accessible entry point before progressing to a barbell hinge. It pairs well with squat-pattern training to build a balanced lower body.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand holding a dumbbell in each hand at your thighs.
2. Keep a slight bend in your knees throughout the movement.
3. Push your hips back as you lower the dumbbells down your legs.
4. Keep the dumbbells close to your legs as they travel down.
5. Lower until you feel a deep stretch in your hamstrings.
6. Drive your hips forward to return to standing.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep the dumbbells in contact with your legs throughout
> — Push your hips back rather than bending your knees forward
> — Keep your chest tall and shoulders pulled back
> — Stop lowering once your lower back wants to round

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The lower back rounds as the dumbbells move below the knees
> — The knees bend too much, turning the hinge into a squat
> — The dumbbells drift forward away from the legs

**Progressions:** Barbell Romanian Deadlift
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Kettlebell Deadlift, Good Morning

## Section 3 — Sumo Deadlift

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Sumo Deadlift |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BARBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | LOWER_BACK, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, QUADS |

**ABOUT**
> The Sumo Deadlift is a barbell hip-hinge exercise performed with a wide stance and hands gripping the bar inside the knees. It trains the glutes, hamstrings, and inner thighs while keeping the torso more upright than a standard Deadlift. The wide stance changes the leverage and muscle emphasis of the lift.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Sumo Deadlift builds total-body pulling strength with a more upright torso position than the standard Deadlift, which can reduce lower-back demand for some lifters. It develops glute and inner-thigh strength alongside the same posterior-chain benefits as any deadlift variation. It is well suited for lifters with longer torsos or those managing a lower-back limitation in the standard stance. It also offers competitive lifters a second deadlift style to specialize in.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet wider than shoulder width, toes pointed outward.
2. Grip the bar with your hands inside your knees.
3. Drop your hips, flatten your back, and pull your shoulders back.
4. Brace your core and take a deep breath in.
5. Drive through your feet to lift the bar, keeping it close to your legs.
6. Stand fully upright with your hips and knees locked out.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Push your knees out in line with your toes throughout the pull
> — Keep the bar close to your shins and thighs the whole lift
> — Keep your back flat from setup through lockout
> — Finish by squeezing your glutes at the top

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The knees cave inward as the bar leaves the floor
> — The hips rise faster than the chest at the start of the lift
> — The lower back rounds as the bar gets heavier

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern barbell variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Dumbbell Deadlift
**Alternatives:** Deadlift, Kettlebell Deadlift

## Section 4 — Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | DUMBBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | LOWER_BACK, CORE |

**ABOUT**
> The Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift is a hip-hinge exercise performed balancing on one leg while lowering a dumbbell toward the floor. It trains the hamstrings and glutes on one side while demanding significant balance. The non-working leg extends behind the body for counterbalance.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift builds hamstring and glute strength on each side independently, exposing and correcting imbalances a bilateral hinge can hide. It carries over to single-leg balance and stability needed in sport and daily movement. It is well suited for lifters who have mastered the standard Romanian Deadlift and want a more demanding balance challenge. It also reinforces hip control that protects the lower back during uneven loading.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand on one leg holding a dumbbell in the opposite hand.
2. Keep a slight bend in your standing knee.
3. Push your hips back as you extend your free leg behind you.
4. Lower the dumbbell toward the floor as your torso tips forward.
5. Lower until you feel a stretch in your standing leg's hamstring.
6. Drive your hips forward to return to standing.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your hips square to the front throughout the movement
> — Move slowly to maintain balance through the full range
> — Keep the dumbbell close to your standing leg
> — Brace your core to stay stable on one leg

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips rotate open as the torso tips forward
> — The standing knee caves inward to compensate for balance
> — The lower back rounds as the dumbbell lowers

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Barbell Romanian Deadlift
**Alternatives:** Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift, Single-Leg Hip Thrust

## Section 5 — Kettlebell Swing

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Kettlebell Swing |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | KETTLEBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | GLUTES, HAMSTRINGS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | LOWER_BACK, CORE |

**ABOUT**
> The Kettlebell Swing is a hip-hinge exercise performed by hinging at the hips to swing a kettlebell between the legs and then driving it forward to shoulder height. It trains the glutes and hamstrings through a fast, repeated hip-hinge motion. It combines strength and conditioning in a single movement.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Kettlebell Swing builds explosive hip-hinge power and posterior-chain endurance in a way slower hinge lifts do not. It is well suited for athletes wanting a conditioning element alongside lower-body strength work. It also reinforces the same hip-snap power used in jumping and sprinting. It is efficient for combining strength and cardiovascular demand in one exercise.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, holding the kettlebell with both hands.
2. Hinge at your hips to swing the kettlebell back between your legs.
3. Brace your core and drive your hips forward explosively.
4. Let the kettlebell swing up to shoulder height as your hips snap forward.
5. Let the kettlebell fall back naturally into the next hinge.
6. Repeat the swing for the set without pausing at the top.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Drive the movement with your hips rather than your arms
> — Keep your back flat throughout the entire swing
> — Let the kettlebell's momentum carry it rather than lifting with your shoulders
> — Snap your hips forward sharply at the bottom of each swing

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The arms lift the kettlebell instead of the hips driving the swing
> — The lower back rounds as the kettlebell swings back
> — The knees bend excessively, turning the hinge into a squat

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Kettlebell Deadlift
**Alternatives:** Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift, Good Morning

## Section 6 — Good Morning

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Good Morning |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BARBELL |
| Primary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, LOWER_BACK |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | GLUTES |

**ABOUT**
> The Good Morning is a barbell hip-hinge exercise performed with the bar resting across the upper back, bending forward at the hips. It trains the hamstrings, glutes, and lower back through a controlled forward bend. It uses lighter loads than a Deadlift or Romanian Deadlift due to the demanding leverage.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Good Morning builds hamstring and lower-back strength through a uniquely demanding leverage position. It reinforces the same hip-hinge mechanics used in the Deadlift and Romanian Deadlift, making it a useful technique-building accessory. It is well suited for lifters looking to strengthen the lower back and hamstrings without adding heavy axial load. It also helps identify hip-hinge technique flaws since the lighter load exposes poor positioning quickly.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Position a lightly loaded bar across your upper back.
2. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart and a slight bend in your knees.
3. Brace your core and push your hips back as you bend forward.
4. Lower your torso until it is close to parallel with the floor.
5. Keep your back flat throughout the forward bend.
6. Drive your hips forward to return to standing.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Use a much lighter load than you would for a Deadlift
> — Push your hips back rather than bending your knees forward
> — Keep your back flat throughout the entire movement
> — Stop bending forward once your lower back wants to round

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The lower back rounds as the torso bends forward
> — The load used is too heavy for the demanding leverage
> — The knees bend significantly, turning the hinge into a squat

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern barbell variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Cable Pull-Through
**Alternatives:** Barbell Romanian Deadlift, Deadlift

## Section 7 — Glute Bridge

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Glute Bridge |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS |

**ABOUT**
> The Glute Bridge is a bodyweight hip-hinge exercise performed lying on the back, driving the hips upward using only body weight. It trains the glutes through a basic hip-extension pattern. It requires no equipment and can be performed almost anywhere.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Glute Bridge builds basic glute activation and hip-extension strength without any equipment, making it the most accessible hinge-pattern exercise in the library. It is well suited for beginners learning to feel the glutes work before progressing to loaded hip-hinge lifts. It also serves as a useful warm-up activation drill before heavier lower-body training. It is low-impact and appropriate for nearly any training level.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat on the floor.
2. Brace your core and squeeze your glutes.
3. Drive your hips up toward the ceiling.
4. Squeeze your glutes hard at the top of the movement.
5. Lower your hips back down under control.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Drive through your heels as you push your hips up
> — Pause and squeeze your glutes at the top of each rep
> — Keep your ribs down rather than arching your lower back
> — Lower with control rather than dropping your hips

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The lower back arches excessively at the top of the movement
> — The heels lift off the floor during the drive up
> — The hips rise unevenly from side to side

**Progressions:** Hip Thrust
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Single-Leg Hip Thrust, Wall Sit

## Section 8 — Cable Pull-Through

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Cable Pull-Through |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | CABLE_MACHINE |
| Primary Muscle(s) | GLUTES, HAMSTRINGS |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | LOWER_BACK |

**ABOUT**
> The Cable Pull-Through is a hip-hinge exercise performed facing away from a low cable machine, hinging forward to pull a rope attachment between the legs. It trains the glutes and hamstrings under constant cable tension. The cable resistance changes through the range differently than a free weight.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Cable Pull-Through builds hip-hinge strength under constant tension, which keeps the glutes and hamstrings working throughout the entire range of motion. It is well suited as a technique-friendly entry point for athletes still learning the hip hinge, since the cable guides a consistent path. It also offers a lower-skill alternative to barbell hinge work while still training the same muscles. It pairs well with squat-pattern training to round out a lower-body program.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Attach a rope handle to a low cable pulley and face away from the machine.
2. Grip the rope between your legs and step forward to create tension.
3. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart and a slight bend in your knees.
4. Push your hips back as you hinge forward, letting the rope travel toward the floor.
5. Lower until you feel a stretch in your hamstrings.
6. Drive your hips forward to return to standing.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Push your hips back rather than bending your knees forward
> — Keep your back flat throughout the entire hinge
> — Let the cable pull your hands back naturally as you hinge
> — Squeeze your glutes at the top of each rep

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The lower back rounds as the torso hinges forward
> — The knees bend too much, turning the hinge into a squat
> — The arms pull the rope instead of the hips driving the motion

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern cable variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Glute Bridge
**Alternatives:** Barbell Romanian Deadlift, Good Morning

## Section 9 — Single-Leg Hip Thrust

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Single-Leg Hip Thrust |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, CORE |

**ABOUT**
> The Single-Leg Hip Thrust is a hip-hinge exercise performed with the upper back against a bench, driving the hips up using one leg at a time. It trains the glutes on each side independently. The non-working leg is held off the floor throughout the movement.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Single-Leg Hip Thrust builds glute strength on each side independently, exposing and correcting imbalances a bilateral Hip Thrust can hide. It carries over to single-leg power needed in sprinting and change-of-direction sports. It is well suited for lifters who have mastered the bilateral Hip Thrust and want a more demanding glute challenge. It also requires no equipment beyond a bench, making it accessible almost anywhere.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Sit on the floor with your upper back against a bench.
2. Plant one foot flat on the floor, knee bent.
3. Extend your other leg straight out in front of you.
4. Brace your core and drive your hips up toward the ceiling.
5. Squeeze your glute hard at the top of the movement.
6. Lower your hips back down under control.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep your hips level throughout the movement
> — Drive through your planted heel as you push up
> — Keep your extended leg steady rather than letting it drop
> — Pause and squeeze your glute at the top of each rep

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hips rotate to one side as they rise
> — The lower back arches excessively at the top
> — The planted heel lifts off the floor during the drive

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Hip Thrust
**Alternatives:** Glute Bridge, Single-Leg Romanian Deadlift

## Section 10 — Back Extension

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Back Extension |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | HINGE |
| Equipment Tag(s) | MACHINE |
| Primary Muscle(s) | LOWER_BACK |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | GLUTES, HAMSTRINGS |

**ABOUT**
> The Back Extension is a machine-based hip-hinge exercise performed by bending forward at the hips over a padded support and raising the torso back up. It trains the glutes, hamstrings, and lower back. The machine supports the legs and hips, isolating the hinge motion.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Back Extension builds glute, hamstring, and lower-back strength in a supported, controlled position. It is well suited for lifters looking to add posterior-chain volume without the technical demands of a barbell hinge. It also offers a useful option for athletes managing a lower-back limitation, since the pad supports the hips throughout. It is a common accessory exercise in hypertrophy and lower-body programs.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Position your hips against the pad with your legs secured behind you.
2. Cross your arms over your chest or hold them behind your head.
3. Brace your core and bend forward at your hips.
4. Lower until you feel a stretch in your hamstrings.
5. Squeeze your glutes to raise your torso back to a flat line.
6. Avoid hyperextending at the top of the movement.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Hinge from your hips rather than rounding your lower back
> — Squeeze your glutes to drive the upward movement
> — Move at a controlled pace rather than swinging up
> — Stop at a flat line rather than arching past it

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The lower back rounds at the bottom of the movement
> — The torso swings up using momentum instead of glute drive
> — The top position hyperextends past a flat line

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern machine variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Glute Bridge
**Alternatives:** Hip Thrust, Good Morning

**ABOUT**
> The Kettlebell Deadlift is a hip-hinge exercise performed by lifting a kettlebell from the floor to hip height. It trains the glutes and hamstrings through a beginner-friendly hinge pattern. It uses a single kettlebell positioned between or in front of the feet.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Kettlebell Deadlift builds basic hip-hinge strength using a single, manageable implement, making it an accessible entry point to deadlift mechanics. It is well suited for beginners learning to hinge correctly before progressing to a barbell. It also serves as a quick warm-up or conditioning option in kettlebell-based training. **HOW TO DO IT**

2. Bend at your hips and knees to grip the kettlebell handle.
3. Drop your hips, flatten your back, and brace your core.

5. Stand fully upright with your hips locked out.

**COACHING CUES**

> — Push the floor away with your feet rather than yanking the kettlebell
> — Keep your back flat from setup through lockout

**WATCH OUT FOR**

**ABOUT**

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Banded Hip Thrust builds glute strength and activation, with the added band resistance encouraging the knees to stay pressed outward throughout the movement. It is well suited for home training or anyone without barbell access. It also reinforces the hip-abduction control that protects the knees during squatting and lunging. It pairs well with bodyweight or dumbbell-based lower-body programs.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Sit on the floor with your upper back against a bench.
2. Place a resistance band around your thighs, just above your knees.
3. Plant your feet flat on the floor, knees bent.
4. Brace your core and drive your hips up, pressing your knees out against the band.
5. Squeeze your glutes hard at the top of the movement.
6. Lower your hips back down under control.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Press your knees out against the band throughout the movement
> — Drive through your heels as you push your hips up
> — Keep your chin tucked and eyes forward
> — Pause and squeeze your glutes at the top of each rep

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The knees cave inward against the band resistance
> — The lower back arches excessively at the top
> — The heels lift off the floor during the drive up

## Section 14 — Broad Jump

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Broad Jump |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | PLYOMETRIC_LOWER |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | QUADS, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS, CALVES |

**ABOUT**
> The Broad Jump is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by jumping forward for maximum horizontal distance. It trains the glutes, quads, and hamstrings for explosive horizontal power. It requires no equipment beyond open floor space.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Broad Jump builds horizontal explosive power that carries over to sprinting starts and any sport requiring forward acceleration. It teaches the body to produce force quickly in a forward direction rather than only vertically. It is well suited for athletes adding a horizontal power component to their training. It also reinforces safe landing mechanics under control.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart at your starting line.
2. Bend your knees and swing your arms back to load the jump.
3. Swing your arms forward and jump explosively forward for distance.
4. Land softly on both feet with bent knees.
5. Stabilize your landing before standing up fully.
6. Reset your stance before the next rep.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Swing your arms forward forcefully to add momentum
> — Land softly with bent knees to absorb the impact
> — Reset your stance fully between reps
> — Aim for a soft, controlled landing rather than maximum distance every rep

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The knees cave inward on landing
> — The landing is stiff and loud instead of soft and controlled
> — The athlete stumbles forward on landing instead of stabilizing

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Pogo Hop
**Alternatives:** Box Jump, Skater Hop

## Section 15 — Lateral Bound

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Lateral Bound |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | PLYOMETRIC_LOWER |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | QUADS, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS |

**ABOUT**
> The Lateral Bound is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by jumping sideways from one leg and landing on the other. It trains the glutes and quads for explosive lateral power. It requires no equipment beyond open floor space.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Lateral Bound builds explosive lateral power and single-leg landing control that carries over to change-of-direction sports. It teaches the body to absorb and redirect force sideways, a demand vertical and forward jumps do not train. It is well suited for athletes in sports requiring quick lateral movement. It also reinforces ankle and knee stability on a single-leg landing.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand balanced on one leg with a slight bend in the knee.
2. Bend your knee and swing your arms to load the jump.
3. Push off explosively to one side, jumping laterally.
4. Land softly on the opposite leg with a bent knee.
5. Stabilize the landing before the next jump.
6. Continue bounding side to side for the set.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Land softly with a bent knee to absorb the impact
> — Stabilize fully on each landing before bounding again
> — Keep your chest up throughout the jump
> — Swing your arms in the direction of the jump for momentum

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The landing knee caves inward on impact
> — The landing is rushed instead of stabilized before the next bound
> — The hips rotate excessively during the jump

**Progressions:** Skater Hop
**Regressions:** Pogo Hop
**Alternatives:** Broad Jump, Jump Squat

## Section 17 — Depth Jump

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Depth Jump |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | PLYOMETRIC_LOWER |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT, BENCH |
| Primary Muscle(s) | QUADS, GLUTES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | CALVES |

**ABOUT**
> The Depth Jump is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by stepping off a box and immediately jumping upward upon landing. It trains the glutes, quads, and hamstrings for rapid force production right after impact. It is one of the most advanced plyometric exercises in the catalog.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Depth Jump builds the ability to absorb force and redirect it into an explosive jump almost instantly, a quality known as reactive strength. It carries over directly to sprinting, jumping, and any sport requiring fast direction changes under impact. It is well suited only for athletes who have already built a solid base of jumping and landing mechanics. It is one of the most demanding lower-body power exercises in the library and should be used sparingly.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand on top of a low box or bench.
2. Step off the box, landing on both feet with bent knees.
3. Absorb the landing briefly with minimal ground contact time.
4. Immediately drive upward into a vertical jump.
5. Land softly again with bent knees after the jump.
6. Reset fully before the next repetition.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep ground contact as brief as possible between landing and jumping
> — Land softly with bent knees on both the step-off and the jump
> — Choose a low box height to keep the landing manageable
> — Reset your stance fully between repetitions

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The knees cave inward during either landing
> — The pause between landing and jumping becomes too long
> — The box height is too aggressive for a controlled landing

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Jump Squat
**Alternatives:** Box Jump, Single-Leg Box Jump

| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |

| Secondary Muscle(s) | HAMSTRINGS |

**ABOUT**
> The Skater Hop is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by continuously bounding side to side from one leg to the other. It trains the glutes and quads for repeated lateral power. It is performed as a continuous flowing series of hops rather than a single bound.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Skater Hop builds repeated lateral power and single-leg landing control across a sustained series of hops. It carries over to skating, lateral cutting, and any sport requiring continuous side-to-side movement. It is well suited for athletes who have mastered the single Lateral Bound and are ready for continuous bounding. It also builds conditioning alongside power, since the hops are performed in a continuous sequence.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand balanced on one leg with a slight bend in the knee.
2. Push off explosively to hop sideways onto the opposite leg.
3. Land softly and immediately push off in the other direction.
4. Continue bounding side to side without pausing between hops.
5. Keep your chest up throughout the sequence.
6. Slow down and stop under control at the end of the set.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep the hops flowing continuously rather than pausing on each landing
> — Land softly with a bent knee on every hop
> — Keep your chest up throughout the sequence
> — Swing your arms with each hop to add momentum

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The landing knee caves inward on impact
> — The hops slow down and lose rhythm as fatigue builds
> — The hips rotate excessively during each bound

**Progressions:** None — no harder same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Regressions:** Lateral Bound
**Alternatives:** Pogo Hop, Jump Lunge

**ABOUT**
> The Single-Leg Box Jump is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by jumping onto a box using only one leg. It trains the glutes, quads, and hamstrings for explosive single-leg power. It is one of the most demanding lower-body plyometric exercises in the catalog.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Single-Leg Box Jump builds single-leg explosive power and landing control beyond what a bilateral Box Jump trains. It carries over to sprinting, jumping, and any sport where force is produced from one leg at a time. It is well suited only for athletes who have already mastered the standard Box Jump and built single-leg strength elsewhere. It exposes and helps correct side-to-side power imbalances.

> — Land softly with a bent knee to absorb the impact
> — Reset your stance fully between reps
> — Step down rather than jumping back down to the floor

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The landing knee caves inward
> — The box height is too aggressive for a controlled single-leg landing
> — The opposite leg swings excessively to generate momentum

## Section 21 — Pogo Hop

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Canonical Name | Pogo Hop |
| Category | LEGS_AND_GLUTES |
| Movement Pattern | PLYOMETRIC_LOWER |
| Equipment Tag(s) | BODYWEIGHT |
| Primary Muscle(s) | CALVES |
| Secondary Muscle(s) | QUADS |

**ABOUT**
> The Pogo Hop is a bodyweight plyometric exercise performed by hopping repeatedly in place using quick, small movements at the ankles. It trains the calves and lower legs for fast, low-amplitude force production. It is the most basic plyometric exercise in the catalog.

**WHY IT MATTERS**
> The Pogo Hop builds basic reactive ability in the ankles and lower legs before progressing to larger, more demanding jumps. It is well suited as a warm-up activation drill before any plyometric or sprint-based training session. It also serves as a safe entry point for athletes new to jump training. It reinforces quick ground contact, a foundational skill for every other plyometric exercise in the library.

**HOW TO DO IT**
1. Stand with feet hip-width apart and a slight bend in your knees.
2. Keep your arms relaxed at your sides.
3. Hop in place using quick, small bounces from your ankles.
4. Keep ground contact time as brief as possible on each hop.
5. Maintain a steady rhythm for the duration of the set.

**COACHING CUES**
> — Keep the hops small and quick rather than jumping high
> — Stay light on your feet with minimal ground contact time
> — Keep your knees only slightly bent throughout
> — Keep a steady, even rhythm across the set

**WATCH OUT FOR**
> — The hops become large jumps instead of small, quick bounces
> — The ground contact time lengthens as fatigue builds
> — The knees stiffen and stop absorbing any of the impact

**Progressions:** Jump Squat
**Regressions:** None — no easier same-pattern variant exists in the launch catalog for this exercise.
**Alternatives:** Broad Jump, Skater Hop
